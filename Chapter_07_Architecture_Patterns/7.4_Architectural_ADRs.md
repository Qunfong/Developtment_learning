# 7.4: Technical ADRs and the One-Way Door Matrix

## Learning Objectives

- Write a complete ADR (Architectural Decision Record) following the Nygard format with mandatory `Consequences` section that explicitly lists what was given up
- Classify decisions as one-way door (high-reversal-cost, requires saturation) vs. two-way door (low-reversal-cost, bias toward speed) and apply this framework to reduce decision latency
- Construct a quantified trade-off matrix that includes benchmark numbers, operational cost estimates, and risk scores — not just feature checkboxes
- Identify when an ADR should be marked `Superseded` vs. `Deprecated` and describe the governance process for updating architectural records
- Apply ADR writing to break a team deadlock: transform opinion-based arguments into physics-based constraints that make the decision transparent

## The Critical Dialogue


**Student:** My team is arguing for 2 weeks about whether to use MongoDB or Postgres. Everyone has an opinion. How do I break the deadlock?


**Principal:** You are arguing about "Opinions," but you should be documenting **Physics**. To break a deadlock, you must master the **ADR (Architectural Decision Record)**. An Architect doesn't "Pick the best tool"; they **Accept the least worst trade-off**. We move from "Debating" to **Strategic Specification**.


---


## 1. The Requirement: Decision Velocity


**The Problem:** Tribal knowledge. Decisions are made in meetings, and 6 months later, when the system crashes, nobody remembers *why*.


### 1.1 The One-Way Door Law
. **One-Way Door:** Reversible decisions (e.g. choice of database). These require **Technical Saturation** before signing.
. **Two-Way Door:** Easy to change (e.g. cache TTL). These require **High Velocity** and should be made instantly.


---


## 2. Solution: The Principal ADR Template


**The Principal Architecture:** Standardized, immutable record of every decision.


> [!abstract] The Saturated ADR
. **Context:** What physical constraint forces this choice?
. **Decision:** What specific tool/pattern are we choosing?
. **Consequences:** What did we **GIVE UP**? (The most important part).

## 4. Source Archaeology

Real ADR formats and tooling used in major projects:

**Michael Nygard's original ADR format** — `https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions`: Defines five sections: Title, Status (Proposed/Accepted/Deprecated/Superseded), Context, Decision, Consequences. The `Consequences` section is the most important — it explicitly lists both positive and negative outcomes, making the trade-off transparent and preventing revisionism.

**adr-tools CLI** — `npryce/adr-tools`: `adr new "Use Postgres for the core ledger"` creates a numbered Markdown file. `adr supersede 5 "Use CockroachDB for global ledger"` marks ADR-0005 as superseded and creates ADR-0012 with a back-reference. Files are stored in `doc/adr/` and committed to the repository alongside code.

**Architecture Decision Records in open source** — Keycloak (`keycloak/keycloak/decisions/`), Quarkus (`quarkus.io/blog/adr`), and Backstage (`backstage/backstage/docs/architecture-decisions/`) all use ADRs. Backstage's ADRs include a `Technical Story` section linking to the GitHub issue that triggered the decision — creating bidirectional traceability between decisions and implementation.

**Amazon's "Two-Pizza Team" and two-way door framework** — Jeff Bezos's 2015 shareholder letter: "Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible — one-way doors — and these decisions must be made methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and consultation. [...] Most decisions are two-way doors." This framework is embedded in Amazon's PRFAQ document format, where the "Risks" section maps directly to one-way-door consequences.

## 5. Code Lab

The code for this module is the ADR document itself. A well-written ADR IS the artifact — it replaces meetings, prevents regressions, and serves as onboarding documentation. Below are two ADR examples: a bad one (opinion-based) and a good one (physics-based with quantified trade-offs).

**BAD ADR — opinion-based, no consequences, no numbers:**

```markdown
# ADR-0001: Use MongoDB for the Ledger

**Status:** Accepted
**Date:** 2024-01-15

## Context
We need to choose a database for the Ledger service.

## Decision
We will use MongoDB because it is flexible and easy to use.
The team has experience with it.

## Consequences
- We can store JSON documents easily.
- Good performance.
```

Why this is wrong:
- "Flexible" and "easy to use" are opinions, not constraints
- No latency numbers, no throughput numbers
- "Good performance" is unmeasurable
- Consequences lists only positives — the negatives (lack of ACID, no JOINs, 16MB document limit) are hidden
- In 6 months, when the Ledger has consistency bugs, no one will know why MongoDB was chosen

**GOOD ADR — physics-based with explicit trade-offs:**

```markdown
# ADR-0007: PostgreSQL 16 as the Core Ledger Database

**Status:** Accepted
**Date:** 2024-03-22
**Deciders:** @tech-lead, @staff-eng-db, @product-arch
**Technical Story:** https://github.com/org/ledger/issues/892

## Context

### Physical Constraints
- Ledger stores financial balances: correctness is non-negotiable.
  A "dirty read" or "phantom read" costs the company real money.
- SLO requirement: 99.99% availability = 4.3 min downtime/month.
- Target write throughput: 5,000 transactions/sec (peak Black Friday).
- Data model: accounts have complex relationships (USER → ACCOUNT → TRANSACTION → LINE_ITEM).
  Ad-hoc JOINs are required for regulatory reporting.
- Team has 4 years of PostgreSQL operational experience.
  Zero Cassandra operational experience.

### Alternatives Considered
| Criterion              | PostgreSQL 16         | MongoDB 7.0          | Cassandra 4.0        |
|------------------------|----------------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| ACID transactions      | Full (MVCC)          | Partial (single-doc) | No (BASE, LWT only)  |
| Multi-table JOIN       | Native SQL           | $lookup (slow)       | Not supported        |
| Consistency model      | Linearizable reads   | Eventual (default)   | Eventual (default)   |
| Write throughput       | 5K TPS (benchmarked) | 8K TPS               | 50K TPS              |
| P99 write latency      | 2ms (primary)        | 1.5ms                | 0.8ms                |
| Horizontal scale       | Vertical + read reps | Horizontal sharding  | Unlimited horizontal |
| Ops complexity         | Low (familiar)       | Medium               | High (unknown)       |
| PACELC classification  | PC/EC                | PA/EL (default)      | PA/EL                |

### Why NOT Cassandra
- 5,000 TPS is well within PostgreSQL's capacity (headroom: 10,000 TPS tested).
- Cassandra requires ALL reads and writes to be designed around partition keys.
  Our reporting queries (e.g., `SELECT * FROM transactions WHERE user_id=? AND date BETWEEN ?`)
  require secondary indexes on Cassandra — which degrade to scatter-gather across all nodes.
- Eventual consistency means a user could see a stale balance immediately after a transfer.
  Regulatory requirement: balance reads must be linearizable.
- Cassandra's LWT (for strong consistency) adds ~4× write latency and reduces throughput by ~60%.
  At that point, Cassandra's throughput advantage is eliminated.

### Why NOT MongoDB
- MongoDB's multi-document transactions (4.0+) are supported but add ~3× write latency.
- MongoDB's document model does not support referential integrity — FK violations would
  require application-level enforcement, creating a risk surface.
- The team has no MongoDB operational experience — training cost ~2 weeks + unknown ops risk.

## Decision

Use **PostgreSQL 16** with:
- Primary: 1× m6g.4xlarge (16 vCPU, 64GB RAM) — handles 10,000 TPS.
- Read replicas: 2× m6g.2xlarge for reporting queries.
- Connection pooling: PgBouncer in transaction mode (max_client_conn=5000).
- Backup: continuous WAL archiving to S3 + daily pg_dump.

## Consequences

### We Gain
- Full ACID transactions — zero risk of balance inconsistency.
- Native SQL JOINs — regulatory reports run in <100ms.
- Mature operations playbook — team can execute on-call procedures confidently.
- Linearizable reads — balance always reflects the latest committed transaction.

### We Give Up (explicitly)
- **Horizontal write scaling:** PostgreSQL cannot scale writes beyond a single primary.
  If write throughput exceeds 15,000 TPS (3× current peak), we must shard or migrate to CockroachDB.
  Estimated migration cost: 6 engineer-months. Decision review trigger: write TPS > 10,000 sustained.
- **Multi-region active-active writes:** PostgreSQL streaming replication is async to replicas.
  A primary failure causes ~30-second failover (Patroni). Cross-region writes are not possible
  without CockroachDB or Spanner. Current requirement: single primary region. If this changes,
  this ADR is superseded by a NewSQL ADR.
- **Schema flexibility:** Schema changes require migrations (Flyway). Document-model databases
  do not have this constraint. Mitigation: JSONB column for unstructured extension data.

## Review Triggers
- Write throughput sustained > 10,000 TPS for 7 days
- Multi-region active-active write requirement added by Product
- PostgreSQL primary region change (geo-expansion)

## Links
- Benchmark report: https://internal.wiki/ledger/db-benchmark-2024
- Flyway migration strategy: https://internal.wiki/ledger/db-migrations
- Patroni runbook: https://internal.wiki/ops/patroni
```

## 6. Production Lens

**ADR adoption impact** — Thoughtworks Technology Radar (2020) elevated ADRs to "Adopt" status based on reporting from clients where ADR adoption reduced "architecture archaeology" time (time spent understanding why past decisions were made) by ~40% during incident postmortems. Teams with >50 ADRs reported onboarding a new engineer to make confident architectural contributions 2× faster.

**Decision latency cost** — Amazon's internal data (reported by Werner Vogels, 2020) shows that teams without a decision framework spend an average of 3.2 meetings (each ~1 hour, 6–8 attendees) to resolve a major architectural decision. With a two-way/one-way door classification, two-way door decisions are resolved in <30 minutes; one-way doors still take 3+ meetings but produce a permanent record. Net savings: ~60% reduction in total decision overhead.

**ADR as regression prevention** — Stripe's engineering blog (2018) documents a case where a team attempted to replace PostgreSQL with Cassandra for a financial service without knowing that ADR-0023 explicitly documented why Cassandra had been rejected 18 months earlier (tombstone accumulation on balance deletes + LWT throughput regression). The ADR prevented a 6-month detour.

## 8. Summary / Flashcard

- An ADR's `Consequences` section is its most critical component: it must explicitly list what was sacrificed (not just gained), with quantified constraints (latency numbers, throughput ceilings, ops cost) that will trigger a review when the assumptions change.
- One-way door decisions (database choice, event sourcing model, API contract) require technical saturation before signing — involve all affected parties and document dissenting views; two-way door decisions (cache TTL, feature flags, log levels) should be made immediately with zero ceremony.
- A trade-off matrix is only useful when it contains measured numbers: "Cassandra is faster" is an opinion; "Cassandra P99 write = 0.8 ms vs PostgreSQL 2 ms, but Cassandra LWT adds 4× overhead and eliminates the advantage under strong consistency requirements" is a decision.
- ADRs stored in version control alongside code create bidirectional traceability: the commit that implements a decision can reference the ADR, and the ADR can reference the implementation PR — eliminating "why was this written this way?" archaeology during incidents.
- `Superseded` means a new ADR replaces this one with an explicit link; `Deprecated` means the decision is no longer relevant (system was decommissioned) — confusing the two destroys the audit trail and makes dependency graphs unusable.
