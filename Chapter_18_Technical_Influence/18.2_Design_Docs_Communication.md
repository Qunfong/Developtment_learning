# 18.2 Design Docs and Communication

---

## 1. LEARNING OBJECTIVES

- Select the correct document type (RFC, Design Doc, or ADR) for a given decision using the decision tree.
- Structure a design doc with an audience-layered format so that tech leads, senior engineers, and future readers each find what they need at the right depth.
- Apply the diagram economy principle by identifying which single diagram carries the most decision-relevant information and eliminating redundant ones.
- Write a design doc that preemptively addresses the three most likely objections from a skeptical senior reviewer.
- Evaluate a design doc for the five major anti-patterns and rewrite the weakest section.

---

## 2. PREREQUISITES

- Chapter 18.1 (RFC Writing and Adoption) — the RFC section template and process concepts are assumed knowledge here. Design docs share the alternatives and trade-offs discipline but serve a different scope.
- Chapter 4 (System Design) — familiarity with architecture diagrams, component boundaries, and sequence diagrams is required for the diagram economy section.
- Chapter 12 (Engineering Leadership) — understanding of how technical documents are consumed by different stakeholders at different levels of abstraction.
- You should have written or reviewed at least one technical document that was read by more than two people. This module is about the moment when your document stops being read by the people you know and starts being read by people you have never met.

## 3. THE CRITICAL DIALOGUE

**Setting:** One-on-one between student and principal. The student has been preparing a design document for a proposed Kafka migration. It is 23 pages long. It has been in review for two weeks. Three people have left comments on page 14 and nobody has commented on the decision itself.

---

**Student:** I spent two weeks on this design doc. It's thorough, it covers everything — the Kafka cluster topology, the consumer group configuration, the exactly-once semantics implementation, the partition strategy. Three people left comments but they're all on the infrastructure details in section 6. Nobody commented on whether we should even do this. I feel like nobody read it.

**Principal:** How long is your executive summary?

**Student:** I don't have one. The first section is Background.

**Principal:** There's your problem. You wrote a document for engineers who are already convinced the decision is right and want to help with implementation. You didn't write a document for the people who need to decide whether the decision is right. Those are two different audiences and they need two different entry points into the same document.

**Student:** But everyone on the team is a senior engineer. They can read the whole thing.

**Principal:** Senior engineers are busy. Your tech lead has four other ongoing design reviews, two hiring interviews this week, and an escalation to deal with. They will read the first page before deciding whether the rest of the document deserves their attention. If your first page is "Background: Our system currently uses synchronous HTTP calls between services," you have told them nothing they don't already know. You have not answered the one question they are asking: "Should I care about this document right now?"

The executive summary does one job: it answers "what decision is being made and what is the recommendation?" in four to six sentences. It is written for someone who has six minutes. The rest of the document is written for someone who has sixty minutes and wants to understand the reasoning. Your conclusion, your consequences, your risk — all visible in the executive summary without requiring any scrolling.

**Student:** So the details in section 6 — the partition strategy, the consumer group config — where do those go?

**Principal:** They stay in the document. But they move to an appendix or a detailed technical section that is explicitly labeled "For engineers implementing this change." Your tech lead does not need to approve your partition count. They need to approve the decision to use Kafka. Those are separate concerns and they belong in separate parts of the document. The fact that the only comments you got are on the implementation details tells you that the implementation-detail readers — the senior engineers who will do the work — found those sections and engaged with them. The decision-maker layer of the document failed because it doesn't exist.

**Student:** What about diagrams? I have six diagrams in the document — current state, proposed state, sequence diagram for publishing, sequence diagram for consuming, failure modes, and the cluster topology.

**Principal:** Six diagrams for one decision is almost always too many. Here is the question you should ask for each diagram: "Would a reviewer's opinion of the core decision change if they did not see this diagram?" For the six you listed: the current state diagram is useful if the current state is non-obvious. The proposed state diagram is essential — it shows what you're building. The two sequence diagrams are probably redundant with each other unless the publishing and consuming paths have a materially different failure story. The failure modes diagram might be critical if your whole argument depends on a specific failure recovery property. The cluster topology diagram belongs in the appendix; nobody needs to approve a cluster topology to approve a Kafka adoption decision.

That leaves you with probably two or three diagrams in the main body. The rest go to the appendix or get cut. Your document will be easier to read, the decision will be clearer, and the reviewers will comment on the right things.

**Student:** What if a senior engineer challenges the document and says "you should have included more detail about X"?

**Principal:** "More detail about X is in the appendix, section A.3" is a complete answer. The layered structure does not remove detail — it organizes it so that the right detail is findable by the right person. The executive summary is a map, not a substitute for the territory.

## 4. THEORY + DIAGRAMS

### Decision Tree: RFC vs Design Doc vs ADR

These three document types are frequently confused. They serve different scopes and different audiences.

```
START: You need to document a technical decision
         |
         v
Is this decision CROSS-TEAM or does it affect
infrastructure/platform used by other teams?
         |
    YES  |  NO
    |         |
    v         v
Does it           Is it a SIGNIFICANT
require           DESIGN DECISION for
formal review     a single service/feature?
and sign-off?         |
    |             YES  |  NO
    v              |        |
  RFC              v        v
(18.1)         DESIGN     ADR
               DOC        (Architecture
               (18.2)      Decision Record)
                           lightweight, 1 page
```

| Dimension | RFC | Design Doc | ADR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | Cross-team, platform-level | Single service or feature, significant design | Any decision worth recording |
| Length | 3-10 pages | 2-8 pages | Half page to 1 page |
| Audience | Multiple teams, tech leads, platform owners | Team + stakeholders | Team members, future maintainers |
| Lifecycle | Formal review, hard deadline, immutable after acceptance | Review cycle, living doc during design phase, frozen after implementation | Written at decision time, never edited |
| Trigger | "Should we change how all services communicate?" | "How should this service handle async processing?" | "We chose PostgreSQL over MySQL for this service" |

**ADR note:** An ADR (Architecture Decision Record, popularized by Michael Nygard) is distinct from both RFC and Design Doc. It records a decision that has already been made, in the past tense, without extensive alternatives analysis. Its value is purely archival. If you find yourself wanting to write an ADR longer than one page, you should probably be writing a Design Doc or RFC instead.

### Audience-Layered Document Structure

The fundamental insight: a design document has multiple readers with fundamentally different needs, and a flat document satisfies none of them well.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  LAYER 1: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY  (top of document, ~half page)   │
│                                                              │
│  Reader: Tech lead, Engineering Manager, cross-team partner  │
│  Time budget: 6 minutes                                      │
│  They need: Decision, recommendation, top risks, ETA         │
│                                                              │
│  Content:                                                    │
│  - What problem are we solving?                              │
│  - What is the recommendation?                               │
│  - What are the top 2 risks?                                 │
│  - What is the timeline?                                     │
└─────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                  │ "I want to understand the reasoning"
                  v
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  LAYER 2: MAIN BODY  (pages 2-5)                             │
│                                                              │
│  Reader: Senior engineers, implementation team, reviewers    │
│  Time budget: 30-45 minutes                                  │
│  They need: Context, design decision, trade-offs,            │
│             alternatives, consequences                       │
│                                                              │
│  Content:                                                    │
│  - Context and motivation                                    │
│  - Core design decision (with the KEY diagram)               │
│  - Alternatives considered                                   │
│  - Trade-offs and risks                                      │
│  - Consequences (what gets better, what gets harder)         │
└─────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                  │ "I need to implement this"
                  v
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  LAYER 3: APPENDIX  (pages 6+, or linked documents)         │
│                                                              │
│  Reader: Engineers implementing the change, future auditors  │
│  Time budget: As needed                                      │
│  They need: Implementation specifics, configuration,         │
│             benchmarks, detailed diagrams                    │
│                                                              │
│  Content:                                                    │
│  - Detailed diagrams (topology, sequence)                    │
│  - Configuration examples                                    │
│  - Benchmark data                                            │
│  - Migration scripts or rollout plan                         │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Why this ordering matters:** Tech leads who cannot find the decision in the first page will not reach page 4 where you bury it. Engineers who want implementation detail will scan for the appendix. Future readers (onboarding engineers six months later) will read the executive summary and main body and understand the system's architecture without needing to understand its implementation details.

---

### Diagram Economy Principle

A diagram has a cost (it takes time to read and interpret) and a value (it conveys information a reader could not derive from prose alone or could derive only with significant effort).

**High-value diagram types for design docs:**
- Architecture boundary diagram: shows what interacts with what, at the component level. Essential if the proposed change moves or creates a boundary.
- Before/after comparison: a single diagram with "current state" and "proposed state" side by side. Extremely efficient — the reader sees the delta immediately.
- Decision diagram: shows the specific architectural choice being made (e.g., where the Kafka topic sits relative to the services, who produces, who consumes).

**Low-value diagram types (usually belong in the appendix or can be cut):**
- Sequence diagrams for "happy path" flows that are obvious from the architecture diagram.
- Data model diagrams for tables that are already documented in the schema repository.
- Deployment topology diagrams when the deployment strategy is not the subject of the decision.
- Any diagram that the author created to fill space or because they spent time on it, not because a reviewer needs it.

**The one-diagram test:** Before finalizing your design doc, ask: "If I could keep only one diagram, which one would be the most important for a reviewer to evaluate the core decision?" That diagram goes in the main body. All others go to the appendix.

---

### Writing for a Skeptical Reader

A design doc written for an agreeable audience is useless. The people who will implement your system are not agreeable — they will find edge cases you missed. Your job as the author is to anticipate their objections and address them in the document before they ask.

**The three-objection drill:** Before finalizing your design doc, generate the three most likely objections a skeptical senior engineer would raise. For each objection:
1. Is the objection already addressed in the document? If yes, is it easy to find?
2. If not addressed: is it a valid concern you should incorporate into the trade-offs section?
3. If not valid: can you add a one-paragraph pre-emption that explains why this objection does not apply?

Common objections for infrastructure design docs:
- "This adds operational complexity — who's on call for it?"
- "What happens when this fails? What's the blast radius?"
- "Have you benchmarked this, or is the performance improvement theoretical?"
- "What's the rollback plan?"
- "This looks similar to what the platform team is building — have you coordinated with them?"

If your design doc does not address at least three of these, it is not ready for review.

---

### How Design Docs Become Onboarding Material

A design doc written in February becomes an onboarding artifact in August when a new engineer joins and asks "why does this service use Kafka instead of direct HTTP calls?"

The properties that make a design doc durable as onboarding material:
- **Context is explicit:** The doc explains the situation as it existed at decision time, not as the author assumed the reader would know it.
- **The "why" is separated from the "how":** A new engineer reading the doc six months later may see a different implementation than what the doc describes (refactors happen). If the "why" is clear, they can evaluate whether the original reasoning still holds.
- **Trade-offs are honest:** A new engineer who inherits a consequence the original doc predicted will trust the doc. A doc that omitted the consequences loses credibility as a reference.

**Practical habit:** When writing a design doc, add one sentence to the executive summary that is explicitly written for a future reader: "If you are reading this after [date] to understand why the system works this way, the core decision and its reasoning are in Section 2."

---

### Design Doc Anti-Patterns

**Anti-pattern 1: Missing motivation.** The document describes what is being built but not why. A new engineer reading it cannot determine whether the decision is still appropriate if circumstances change.

**Anti-pattern 2: No alternatives.** The document presents one option and calls it the design. This is an implementation plan, not a design document. Without alternatives, there is no evidence the author considered other approaches.

**Anti-pattern 3: Implementation-level detail instead of design-level.** The document specifies Spring bean configurations, SQL migration scripts, and Dockerfile commands. This detail belongs in implementation tickets or the appendix, not in a design doc. Implementation detail in the main body obscures the design decisions reviewers are supposed to evaluate.

**Anti-pattern 4: Written after the decision is already made.** The document is a post-hoc justification for a decision that was made in a meeting or Slack thread. This is detectable because the alternatives section is thin or absent, the trade-offs section mentions no real costs, and the document reads like a press release. This practice is more damaging than writing no document at all, because it creates a false record.

**Anti-pattern 5: Written for the author, not the reader.** The document assumes the reader has the same context as the author. Background is omitted because "everyone knows this." Acronyms are not defined. The problem statement skips the impact because "it's obvious." This document fails the moment it is read by someone who was not in the original conversation.

## 5. SOURCE ARCHAEOLOGY

### Google's Non-Goals Section

Google's internal design doc template, described by multiple ex-Google engineers (most accessibly in Malte Ubl's "Design Docs at Google" article on industrialempathy.com), includes an explicit "Non-Goals" section. This section answers: "What problems are we explicitly not solving with this design?" The value is boundary enforcement: when a reviewer suggests adding a feature to the proposal, the author can point to the non-goals section and say "that's explicitly out of scope for this document." Without an explicit non-goals section, scope creep in design review is nearly inevitable.

### Amazon's Working Backwards Document Structure

Amazon's "Working Backwards" process structures product and technical documents with the press-release-and-FAQ format: the document opens with a hypothetical press release announcing the successful outcome of the project, followed by a FAQ that addresses the toughest questions a skeptical reader would ask. This structure enforces audience-first thinking — the author must write the outcome before writing the design, which surfaces goal misalignment early. The press-release section is functionally equivalent to the executive summary layer described in this module.

### Spotify's System Design Documentation Practices

Spotify's engineering blog (engineering.atspotify.com) describes their practice of "golden paths" — documented, standardized approaches to common architectural decisions. These golden path documents function as pre-written design docs: when a team needs to make a common architectural decision (message queue, cache, search), the golden path document provides the rationale, trade-offs, and constraints, eliminating the need for each team to rediscover the same reasoning. This is the highest form of design doc value: a design doc written once, used by hundreds of teams.

### IETF RFC Abstract and Introduction Structure

IETF RFCs use a strict structure where the Abstract (equivalent to executive summary) must stand alone as a complete summary of the document's content. RFC editors reject abstracts that merely describe what the document is about without stating what it says. This discipline — the abstract must summarize conclusions, not just topics — is directly applicable to design doc executive summaries. A design doc executive summary that says "This document describes our Kafka migration approach" fails the IETF abstract test. One that says "We recommend migrating to Kafka for inter-service messaging to eliminate synchronous coupling; this reduces blast radius during service outages and enables independent scaling. Primary risk: operational complexity of Kafka cluster management." passes.

## 6. CODE LAB

### Worked Example: One-Page Design Doc for Kafka Adoption

The following is a complete, annotated design doc. This is explicitly a one-page (main body) design doc, appropriate for a decision that affects one team and one service boundary. It follows the audience-layered structure.

---

```
Design Doc: Async Inter-Service Messaging via Kafka
Team: Platform Services
Author: [Your Name]
Status: In Review
Date: 2024-04-10
Reviewers: @techLead, @seniorEngineerA, @infrastructureTeam
Decision deadline: 2024-04-17
```

---

**Executive Summary**

We recommend replacing synchronous HTTP calls between the order-service and the notification-service with Kafka-based async messaging. This eliminates a tight coupling that caused 3 production incidents in Q1 2024 when the notification-service was degraded. The change reduces order-service blast radius, enables notification retry without order-service involvement, and improves order throughput by ~15% by removing synchronous wait time. Primary risk: operational overhead of Kafka cluster management, mitigated by using the existing platform-managed Kafka cluster. Target completion: 6 weeks.

**[Annotation: Six sentences. Decision stated. Recommendation stated. Quantified benefit stated. Primary risk stated. Mitigation stated. Timeline stated. A tech lead who reads only this has everything they need to decide whether to approve further design work.]**

---

**Context**

The order-service currently makes a synchronous POST call to the notification-service after each order confirmation. This call is in the critical path: if the notification-service takes longer than 2 seconds or returns a 5xx error, the order confirmation response to the user is delayed or failed.

In Q1 2024, three incidents (INC-1401, INC-1402, INC-1418) involved notification-service latency spikes (caused by email provider throttling) propagating back to order confirmation failures. Combined user impact: 340 failed order confirmations, 2.1% of daily order volume on those days.

**[Annotation: Three sentences of current state, three sentences of quantified impact. The reviewer now knows the problem is real and worth solving. No implementation detail here.]**

---

**Decision**

Use the platform-managed Kafka cluster to publish order-confirmed events from the order-service. The notification-service consumes these events asynchronously. Order confirmation to the user is no longer blocked on notification delivery.

```
CURRENT STATE:
User → [Order Service] --sync POST--> [Notification Service] → Email Provider
                                           ↑ failure propagates back

PROPOSED STATE:
User → [Order Service] --publishes--> [Kafka: order-confirmed]
                                           ↓ async
                               [Notification Service] → Email Provider
                                      (retries independently)
```

**[Annotation: One diagram. It is a before/after showing exactly the architectural change being made. The reader sees the delta immediately. No sequence diagrams, no topology diagrams — those belong in the appendix.]**

---

**Alternatives Considered**

**Circuit breaker on the existing HTTP call:** Add Resilience4j circuit breaker to the notification-service call, returning success to the user when the breaker is open and retrying in background. This solves the blast-radius problem but not the synchronous coupling. If the notification-service is slow (not down), the order-service still waits synchronously. Rejected: does not address the root cause (synchronous dependency on a non-critical side-effect).

**Outbox pattern with direct retry (no Kafka):** Write notification requests to a database outbox table; a scheduler retries failed notifications. Solves the problem without Kafka operational complexity. Rejected: the platform team has explicitly advised against new service-specific scheduled jobs due to inconsistent failure handling across teams; Kafka is the platform standard for this pattern.

**[Annotation: Two real alternatives, both honestly evaluated. The outbox pattern is actually a legitimate solution — the author explains specifically why it was rejected (platform policy, not technical inferiority). A reviewer who prefers the outbox pattern can challenge this rejection cleanly.]**

---

**Trade-offs and Risks**

- Notification delivery is now eventually consistent. The user sees "order confirmed" before the email is sent. This is acceptable for emails (users expect some delay) but would not be acceptable for payment confirmation (which remains synchronous). This constraint must be documented in the service contract.
- Kafka operational dependency: if the Kafka cluster has an outage, notifications are queued (not lost), but order-service must handle the case where Kafka is unavailable. We will implement a fallback: if Kafka publish fails, log the event to the outbox table as a safety net.
- Consumer group management: the notification-service team is responsible for consumer group offset management. They must not commit offsets before processing is complete (at-least-once delivery semantics).

**[Annotation: Three specific costs. Each one has an implication for the engineers who will implement and operate this system. The first trade-off (eventual consistency) even constrains future decisions ("would not be acceptable for payment confirmation"). This is the kind of forward-looking constraint that prevents future engineers from making incorrect analogies.]**

---

**Consequences**

If accepted:
- Order-service p99 confirmation latency decreases by an estimated 150-200ms (the current synchronous notification call).
- Notification delivery failure rate declines because the notification-service can retry independently without affecting users.
- Future services that need to react to order-confirmed events (analytics, fraud detection) can consume the same Kafka topic without modifying the order-service.

If rejected:
- The synchronous coupling remains. The team should evaluate the circuit-breaker alternative more seriously and implement at minimum a timeout and fallback.

**[Annotation: Consequences are separated from the trade-offs section. Consequences describe what changes after acceptance; trade-offs describe what the proposal costs. Including "if rejected" consequences is unusual but valuable — it tells reviewers that rejecting the doc does not make the problem go away.]**

---

**Appendix A: Kafka Topic Configuration (for implementing engineers)**

Topic: `order-confirmed`, partitions: 6, replication factor: 3, retention: 7 days, message format: Avro with schema registry, max.message.bytes: 1MB.

Consumer group: `notification-service-order-consumer`, auto.offset.reset: earliest, enable.auto.commit: false (manual commit after processing).

**[Annotation: Implementation detail in the appendix. The tech lead does not need to approve partition counts. The engineers doing the work need this information. Separation achieved.]**

### Anti-Pattern Gallery: Same Document, Five Ways to Fail It

**Anti-pattern 1 — Missing motivation (context section):**
```
BEFORE (bad):
"Context: We use HTTP between order-service and notification-service."

Why it fails: States what exists. Does not state why it's a problem.

AFTER (good):
"The order-service synchronously calls the notification-service in the
order-confirmation critical path. In Q1 2024, three incidents showed
that notification-service degradation propagates to order failures.
340 order confirmations failed over three incident windows."
```

**Anti-pattern 2 — No alternatives:**
```
BEFORE (bad):
"We will use Kafka for async messaging. This is the industry standard
approach for decoupling services."

Why it fails: Industry standard is not a design rationale. What other
options were considered and why were they worse?

AFTER (good): [the alternatives section shown in the worked example above]
```

**Anti-pattern 3 — Implementation detail masquerading as design:**
```
BEFORE (bad):
"We will create a KafkaProducerConfig bean with
ProducerFactory<String, OrderConfirmedEvent> and configure the
KafkaTemplate with a DefaultKafkaProducerFactory. The topic will
be created with 6 partitions..."

Why it fails: This is implementation. Nobody needs to approve this
in a design review. It belongs in code or the appendix.

AFTER (good):
"The order-service publishes order-confirmed events to Kafka after
successful order persistence. Events use Avro serialization to enable
schema evolution. Details in Appendix A."
```

**Anti-pattern 4 — Written after the decision:**
```
BEFORE (bad):
"After careful analysis, the team has decided to adopt Kafka.
This document describes the approach."

Why it fails: The decision is already made. This is a justification
document, not a design document. It will not receive honest review
because the author is not genuinely open to alternatives.

AFTER (good):
[Status: IN REVIEW] and an alternatives section that genuinely
engages with other options.
```

**Anti-pattern 5 — Assumes shared context:**
```
BEFORE (bad):
"Due to the issues we've been having with notifications, we're
proposing Kafka as a fix."

Why it fails: "The issues we've been having" is invisible to anyone
not in the last three team meetings. A new joiner reading this six
months later has no idea what issues, how severe, how often.

AFTER (good):
"Three incidents in Q1 2024 (INC-1401, INC-1402, INC-1418)..."
```

## 7. PRODUCTION LENS

### Case Study: The 20-Page Document Nobody Read

A fintech startup's payments team was designing a new settlement reconciliation system. The senior engineer responsible for the design produced a comprehensive 22-page design document covering every aspect of the system: database schema, API contract, reconciliation algorithm, retry logic, alerting configuration, runbook procedures, and integration test plan.

The document was sent to five reviewers. Over two weeks, four reviewers left comments — all on pages 14-18, which described the database schema and retry logic. The core architectural decision — whether to use an event-sourcing pattern or a state-machine pattern for reconciliation — received no comments.

The team proceeded with the event-sourcing pattern as described. Eight months into implementation, a new tech lead joined the team and immediately identified that the event-sourcing pattern was a poor fit for the reconciliation use case (reconciliation requires point-in-time state reconstruction, which event sourcing makes expensive). A 6-week refactor followed.

Post-incident analysis of the design review: the original design document's page 1 began with "Background: Settlement reconciliation is the process of ensuring..." — two paragraphs of definitional context that every reviewer already knew. The architectural decision (event sourcing vs state machine) appeared on page 6, buried after context, problem statement, and a full-page architecture diagram of the current system.

The new tech lead, reviewing the original document retroactively, read to page 3 and stopped. The document had passed the point where a 6-minute reader would stop before reaching the decision.

**What the document needed:** An executive summary on page 1 that said: "We recommend event sourcing for settlement reconciliation. The primary architectural trade-off is query performance: reconstructing current state from events is O(n) in event history. This is acceptable given our reconciliation batch window of 4 hours. Alternative: state machine pattern with a single reconciliation-state table. Rejected because it requires distributed locking across services. If you disagree with this trade-off, see Section 3."

A tech lead reading that executive summary would have immediately flagged the O(n) event reconstruction concern before the team spent eight months building on it.

**The lesson:** Design review failure is rarely a reviewer attention problem. It is an author structure problem. The information was in the document. It was not findable in 6 minutes. The cost of that structural failure was a 6-week refactor.

### Case Study: The Design Doc That Prevented a Duplicate System

A mid-size logistics company had two backend teams working independently. Team A wrote a design doc for a real-time package tracking event pipeline. Their executive summary was visible in the company's shared design doc repository.

Four months later, Team B began designing a real-time shipment status update system. A senior engineer on Team B, during the discovery phase, searched the design doc repository for "real-time" and found Team A's document. The executive summary was clear enough that the Team B engineer recognized the overlap in 10 minutes. A conversation between the two teams followed. They discovered that 80% of their requirements were identical, and Team B ended up consuming Team A's event pipeline rather than building a competing one.

Estimated savings: 3 engineering months for Team B, plus the long-term operational savings of maintaining one pipeline instead of two.

**The lesson:** Design docs in searchable repositories function as a broadcast of "here is what we are building." The broadcast only works if the executive summary is clear enough to be recognized by someone who was not in your team meetings.

## 8. EXERCISES

**Exercise 1 — Executive Summary Retrofit (Writing)**
Take a design doc or technical proposal from your current or recent project that does not have an executive summary. Write one for it: four to six sentences, covering decision, recommendation, top two risks, and timeline. Share it with a colleague who was not involved in the original decision. Ask them to tell you what decision was made and what the primary trade-off was. If they cannot answer both questions accurately, revise the executive summary until they can.

**Exercise 2 — Diagram Audit (Writing)**
Take a design doc with three or more diagrams. For each diagram, apply the one-diagram test: "Would a reviewer's opinion of the core design decision change if they did not see this diagram?" Mark diagrams as "main body" or "appendix" based on this test. If more than two diagrams remain in the main body, apply the test again more strictly. Write a one-paragraph justification for each diagram that remains.

**Exercise 3 — Skeptical Reader Simulation (Writing)**
Take a design doc you have recently written or reviewed. Generate the three most likely objections a skeptical senior engineer would raise. For each objection, find where in the document it is addressed. If it is not addressed, add a paragraph addressing it. If it is addressed but hard to find, move the relevant content so it appears before the objection would arise in a linear reading.

**Exercise 4 — Anti-Pattern Hunt (Reflection)**
Review three design docs from your company's documentation system. For each doc, identify which of the five anti-patterns are present (missing motivation, no alternatives, implementation-level detail, written after decision, assumes shared context). Rank the docs from best to worst structured. What patterns do the better docs share that the worse docs lack?

**Exercise 5 — New Joiner Simulation (Reflection)**
Pick a design doc for a system you currently work on. Read it as if you joined the team today with no prior context. After reading only the executive summary and main body (not the appendix), answer: (a) Why does this system work the way it does? (b) What was the alternative that was most seriously considered? (c) What is the main thing you cannot easily change about this system's design? If you cannot answer all three questions, the document fails the onboarding test. Identify what information is missing.


**Colleague test (how to apply it):**
Hand this 4-sentence summary to a colleague who was not involved. Ask two questions:
1. "What decision was made?" → Expected: "Migrate to gRPC for internal services."
2. "What is the primary trade-off?" → Expected: Something about schema overhead / Envoy dependency vs latency gains.

If they cannot answer both questions from the executive summary alone, the summary is not doing its job. The most common failure: the "risks" section is vague ("there may be challenges") or missing entirely, so the reader only learns what will be done, not why it might not work.

**Staff-level phrasing:** "An executive summary that only describes the happy path is not a summary — it is marketing. The primary trade-off and the top two risks are the most important sentences in the document for the person who has to approve it."
</details>

<details>
<summary>Exercise 2 — Diagram Audit</summary>

**One-diagram test:** "Would a reviewer's opinion of the core design decision change if they did not see this diagram?"

**Applied to a typical 6-diagram gRPC migration design doc:**

| Diagram | Decision-critical? | Placement verdict |
|---------|-------------------|------------------|
| Component diagram: current REST topology vs proposed gRPC topology | Yes — shows exactly which services change and which stay REST | **Main body** |
| Sequence diagram: gRPC call flow for the order → payment pair | Yes — demonstrates how the proposal changes the critical path | **Main body** |
| Sequence diagram: error handling and circuit breaker behavior | No — important operational detail, not decision-relevant | **Appendix** |
| Data model: Protobuf schema for `Order` and `Payment` messages | No — the schema is implementation, not design decision | **Appendix** |
| Network topology: which nodes Envoy sits on | No — infrastructure detail | **Appendix** |
| Migration timeline: Gantt chart | No — belongs in the project plan, not the design doc | **Remove** |

**Result:** 2 main-body diagrams, 3 in appendix, 1 removed. This is the typical outcome of applying the test strictly.

**Why two diagrams maximum:**
The reading cost of a diagram is ~3-5 minutes (understanding the notation, following the flows, connecting it to the text). A reviewer reading 6 diagrams spends 18-30 minutes on diagrams alone — more than the main body text. The diagrams that don't change their opinion are stealing attention from the ones that do.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Exercise 3 — Skeptical Reader Simulation</summary>

**The three most likely objections to a gRPC migration proposal:**

**Objection 1: "Why not Protobuf serialization over REST instead of full gRPC?"**

This achieves 60-70% of the performance gain (serialization overhead reduction) with significantly less ecosystem change — no HTTP/2 multiplexing, no new proxy requirements, existing HTTP tooling continues to work. If the doc does not address this, add a paragraph to the Alternatives section explicitly: "We evaluated Protobuf-over-HTTP/1.1 and measured 35% latency reduction in our benchmark vs 40% for full gRPC. We chose gRPC for the additional benefits of streaming, built-in flow control, and server-side push — capabilities we need for the notification service in H2."

**Objection 2: "How do developers debug gRPC in production when binary frames are unreadable?"**

This is an operational concern that affects every team adopting gRPC. If not addressed, teams will discover it during their first production incident. Add to Trade-offs section: "gRPC requires `grpcurl` (a CLI tool) and Envoy admin interface for live debugging. All teams will need to update their HTTP debugging runbooks. We will provide a team training session and a `grpcurl` command cheatsheet before Phase 1 go-live."

**Objection 3: "What is the rollback plan if a service migration fails?"**

Irreversibility is the key question for a protocol migration. Add an explicit Rollback section or Open Question: "Each service migration includes a feature flag on the client side that reverts to REST if the gRPC endpoint returns errors above 1%. Rollback to REST requires 5 minutes (flag flip + deploy). Data format compatibility: the Protobuf schema is designed with the same field names as the current JSON schema — a rollback does not require data migration."

**Staff-level phrasing:** "The skeptical reader simulation is how you find the gaps in your own argument before your audience finds them for you — every objection you generate and address proactively is one less blocker in the review."
</details>

<details>
<summary>Exercise 4 — Anti-Pattern Hunt</summary>

**The five anti-patterns and their signatures:**

| Anti-pattern | Signature in the doc | Consequence |
|-------------|---------------------|-------------|
| **Missing motivation** | Describes what will be built but not why now, why this problem matters, or what it costs to do nothing | Reviewers cannot assess whether the investment is justified — cannot say yes |
| **No alternatives** | Single option presented as "the solution" with no discussion of what else was considered | Cannot distinguish a considered choice from an unconsidered one — kills trust |
| **Implementation-level detail** | 30% of the main body is code snippets, config examples, or deployment steps | Buries the decision in implementation — tech lead cannot read to the end |
| **Written after decision** | All past tense, no open questions, no decision criteria, "we chose X because Y" framing throughout | The document is a justification, not a design tool — provides no decision governance |
| **Assumes shared context** | References internal systems, incidents, or jargon without explanation | New joiners (and future you) cannot use the document — it requires tribal knowledge to decode |

**Ranking heuristic:**
Best docs: missing 0-1 anti-patterns, with "missing motivation" being the most forgivable.
Worst docs: "written after decision + no alternatives" — these co-occur because the author knew the answer before writing and reverse-engineered the document to support it.

**Common finding from real audits:** Most company wikis are 80% anti-pattern 4 (written after decision). The few good docs were written before the decision was final, under time pressure, by engineers who actually needed consensus to proceed.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Exercise 5 — New Joiner Simulation</summary>

**Three questions that a design doc must answer without prior context:**

**(a) Why does this system work the way it does?**

This question tests whether the architectural constraints are documented. If the answer requires reading commit history, asking a senior engineer, or consulting a separate ADR, the design doc fails. The constraints that drove the design (latency requirements, consistency model, organizational structure, prior technology commitments) must be in the Context section.

Common gap: the system uses a particular data store "for historical reasons" that are never documented. The new joiner makes a "better" change that violates the constraint, and discovers the reason in production.

**(b) What was the alternative most seriously considered?**

If not in the doc, the new joiner cannot understand why the current design is not obviously superior to its alternatives. This causes redesign proposals that relitigate decisions already made — wasting engineering time on debates that have already been resolved.

**(c) What is the main thing you cannot easily change?**

This is the irreversibility question. If the doc does not state it, future engineers will discover it through failed refactors. Examples: "the partition key for the Kafka topic cannot be changed without a full consumer rebuild and replay," "the database schema uses composite keys that make the ORM mapping non-standard — changing the entity model requires a data migration."

**Test application workflow:**
1. Read only the executive summary + main body (close the appendix)
2. Answer all three questions from memory
3. For each question you could not answer, find where in the doc that information *should* be
4. If the information exists in the appendix but not the main body, move it
5. If the information doesn't exist anywhere, write it

**Staff-level phrasing:** "A design doc that fails the new joiner test is a document that only works for the people who don't need it — the ones who already know the context. The people who will need it most are the engineers who inherit the system in 18 months."
</details>

## 9. SUMMARY / FLASHCARD

- **Audience-layered structure is mandatory, not optional:** Tech leads read the executive summary (6 minutes). Senior engineers read the main body (45 minutes). Future maintainers read both. Writing a flat document that satisfies none of these reading patterns is the primary reason design docs go unread.

- **Diagram economy: one diagram that changes minds, rest in the appendix:** Every diagram has a reading cost. The only diagrams that belong in the main body are the ones where a reviewer's opinion of the core decision changes if the diagram is absent. Six diagrams for one decision is almost always too many.

- **Anti-patterns signal an author who is selling, not reasoning:** Missing motivation, no alternatives, post-decision justification documents — these signal that the author is not genuinely open to review. Experienced engineers recognize this and their engagement drops accordingly.

- **The executive summary is the most leveraged writing in the document:** A new tech lead who has 6 minutes will decide whether to engage with the full document based on the executive summary. The executive summary must state: what decision is being made, what is recommended, what the top risk is, and what the timeline is.

- **Behavioral signal for Staff-level interviews:** When asked "Tell me about a time your technical proposal was rejected or significantly changed," the strongest answers include: how the document was structured for the right audience, what objections were anticipated in the doc, and what the reviewer interaction loop looked like. An answer that describes a rejected Slack message is not a Staff-level answer.
